What "you pay" means

Each card shows the bike's price minus your rebate. Sales tax is added on top, because the rebate can't be used for tax. It can't be used for shipping either.

How to choose an e-bike

A quick read based on Ava's buying guide.

The three classes

Every e-bike sold in the US is one of three classes. The class is on each card.

Class 1Pedal assist, up to 20 mph

The motor only helps while you pedal. Feels like a regular bike with a boost. The easiest place to start.

Class 2Pedal assist plus throttle, up to 20 mph

Adds a throttle, so you can get moving without pedaling. Handy from a stop or with a heavy load.

Class 3Pedal assist, up to 28 mph

The fastest legal class, with a speedometer. Good for longer trips. In California it can't have a throttle, and it isn't allowed on bike-only off-road trails.

The three types on this list

Standard · $400 offCity and commuter

Lighter and easy to handle. Upright and comfortable for commuting and errands. A great choice unless you need to haul a lot.

Cargo · $900 offBuilt to carry

Room in front or back for kids, groceries or tools, with stronger motors. Some have three wheels. Bigger, heavier and less nimble.

Adaptive · $900 offFor mobility needs

Trikes, recumbents, tandems and options for wheelchair users. Wider, so they take more room on the road and in storage.

What's on the spec sheet

Motor
Hub motors sit in the wheel and feel like a tailwind. Mid-drive motors sit at the pedals and feel more natural, especially on hills. Torque (Nm) is pulling power: more torque helps with hills and heavy loads.
Battery and range
Capacity is in watt-hours (Wh). More Wh means more miles per charge, but a heavier bike. A full charge takes about 1 to 5 hours. Use the charger that came with the bike, plugged straight into the wall, and don't leave it charging for days.
The display
The handlebar screen shows speed and battery, sets the assist level, and runs the throttle if the bike has one.
Max load
The most weight the bike is rated to carry, including you. Leave room for cargo or a passenger on top of your own weight.

Ask yourself first

  • Am I replacing a daily car trip?
  • Will I take it on a bus or train?
  • How many miles a day or week will I ride?
  • What do I need to carry: a laptop, kids, groceries, tools?
  • Who will handle maintenance?
  • Would a low step-through or a third wheel make riding easier?

Then compare on

  • Power: enough for your hills and loads, and right for everyone who will ride it.
  • Fit: frame size, seat and handlebars. Test ride if you can.
  • Weight: pick one you can lift and move yourself.
  • Range: enough battery for your normal day with room to spare.
  • Accessories: racks, fenders, lights, phone mount.
  • Service: a local shop you trust for repairs and parts.
